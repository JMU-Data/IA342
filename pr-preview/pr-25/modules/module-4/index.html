Design Purpose

04 — Color can explain and persuade

A highlight directs attention. A palette can also suggest mood, urgency or identity.

Ask whether that impression fits the evidence and the purpose.

Key point: Color is not merely aesthetic ornamentation. It directs focus, shapes interpretation, and establishes tone. Always evaluate whether the visual emphasis aligns with the underlying analytical evidence and intelligence purpose.

Color Dimensions

11 — Hue, value and saturation

Hue: the color family (wavelength category).

Saturation: intensity or purity — vivid versus neutral gray.

Value: relative lightness or darkness. In data visualization, value carries natural order.

Legibility vs Preference

14 — Color combinations: liking versus reading

Rate "I like it" and "It is easy to read" separately.

Would you still choose a favorite combination for small map labels or multi-series charts?

Core Principle: Aesthetic preference does not predict legibility. High value contrast between foreground mark and background is required for fast, accurate text and symbol discrimination.

Source: Historical legibility tables: Sharpe (1974), Hackl (1981); discussion: Borden D. Dent, Jeffrey S. Torguson and Thomas W. Hodler (2009), Cartography: Thematic Map Design, 6th ed., McGraw-Hill.

Analytical Insight

16 — What can we claim from a color preference game?

Our class may disagree about a pair while agreeing that its labels are hard to read.

Keep liking, emotional association and legibility separate.

Key point: Aesthetic preference is subjective and cultural; legibility is functional and measurable. When designing dashboards and thematic maps, prioritize text contrast and reading speed over personal aesthetic favorites.

Biological Foundations

24 — Cone responses, spectral sensitivity, and why deficiency types differ

Color vision depends on overlapping S, M and L cone responses across the visible spectrum. The cones do not each respond to only one pure color; they respond to overlapping ranges of wavelengths.

That is why a deficiency can happen in more than one way:

  • Dichromacy: one cone class is absent.
  • Anomalous trichromacy: all three cone classes are present, but one response is altered.

This vocabulary makes the simulations on the next slides meaningful: protan-, deutan-, and tritan-type deficiencies affect different cone-response information.

The response curves are a teaching schematic, not measured spectral-sensitivity data. The main point is overlap: color perception is based on comparing cone responses, not on three isolated red/green/blue channels.

Sources: National Eye Institute, "Types of Color Vision Deficiency"; National Eye Institute, "Color Blindness"; Gustavo M. Machado, Manuel M. Oliveira and Leandro A. F. Fernandes (2009), "A Physiologically-based Model for Simulation of Color Vision Deficiency," IEEE TVCG 15(6), 1291–1298. Diagram: original classroom SVG based on these sources.

Design Guidelines

28 — Using color: a practical starting point

  • Avoid relying only on red versus green; try orange versus blue.
  • Use ramps for ordered magnitude and categorical colors for identities.
  • Aim for about five or fewer key categories; add direct labels.
  • Keep backgrounds and color meanings consistent.
Key point: Under five is practical design guidance, not a rigid biological cutoff. Discrete numbers are still quantitative; categorical encoding does not mean assigning a different saturated hue to every unique value.

Source: Steve Wexler, Jeffrey Shaffer and Andy Cotgreave (2017), The Big Book of Dashboards, Wiley, Chapter 1, "Data Visualization: A Primer"; Cynthia A. Brewer, Mark Harrower and The Pennsylvania State University, ColorBrewer 2.0; W3C, WCAG 2.2, Understanding SC 1.4.1: Use of Color.

Cognitive Load

29 — Too much color: the legend can become the task

When many categories each get a saturated hue, the reader may spend more effort decoding the legend than reading the pattern.

Before adding more colors, ask whether grouping, highlighting, direct labels, or small multiples would reduce the lookup burden.

The next activity turns that idea into a quick classroom test.

Key question: If the reader has to look back and forth between the chart and the legend ten times, is the chart doing its job?

Source: design discussion informed by Steve Wexler, Jeffrey Shaffer and Andy Cotgreave (2017), The Big Book of Dashboards, Wiley, Chapter 1, "Data Visualization: A Primer".

Cognitive Experiment

30 — Try four categories. Then ten.

Study the legend for five seconds and identify three colors.

Compare accuracy and notice how often you need to glance back at the legend.

Working Memory Limit: Human visual working memory reliably tracks only 3–5 discrete colors simultaneously. Above 7 categories, the legend becomes an active analytical obstacle.

Source: Original classroom memory activity; related design discussions: Colin Ware (2012), Information Visualization: Perception for Design, 3rd ed., Morgan Kaufmann.

Color Context

31 — Background is part of the color design

Both center squares have identical RGB values.

Reveal the connection; then match the backgrounds to see how surrounding value alters perceived brightness.

Simultaneous Contrast: The eye perceives color relative to its surroundings. A light background makes an identical patch look darker, while a dark background makes it appear brighter.

Source: Simultaneous contrast principle: Josef Albers (1963), Interaction of Color, Yale University Press.

Design Checklist

44 — Before you keep a color, ask why it is there

Use two public originals as a final checklist:

Before keeping a color, ask:

  • Does it encode order, category, selection, magnitude, or a stated condition?
  • Is it mainly creating theme or mood? If so, does it interfere with reading?
  • Is the color meaning consistent across the visual?
  • If color disappeared, could the reader still recover the important information?
  • Does the visual imply a stronger story than the evidence actually supports?

Sources: Jeff's Halloween Metrics; How Does Rain Effect Trick or Treating?; W3C WCAG 2.2 — Use of Color.
