Map Design & Spatial Intelligence

Connecting Evidence, Coordinate Systems, Classification, and Thematic Map Encodings

IA 342: Visualization Methods, Technologies, and Tools for Intelligence Analysis

This lecture explains how maps represent the world, how coordinate systems place data, how color and classification change what people see, and how different thematic map types answer different analytical questions.

Spatial Framing

01 — Why Maps?

"If a picture is worth a thousand words, a map is worth a thousand cells."

Location connects disparate records that originated from different tables, agencies, databases, or physical sensors. A map reveals spatial clusters, gaps, corridors, boundaries, and proximity relationships that are invisible in tabular rows.

In spatial intelligence analysis, we organize and examine data across hierarchical geographic scales: country, state, county, census tract, block group, street segment, or precise coordinates.

Source concept: Instructor Lecture 5, slides 2–4.

Cartographic Definition

03 — Maps Represent Cultural and Physical Environments

A map is a graphic representation of selected parts of the cultural or physical environment.

  • Physical features: terrain topography, hydrology, coastlines, and vegetative cover.
  • Cultural features: transportation networks, political boundaries, critical infrastructure, and place names.
  • Statistical patterns: demographic distribution, median income, health metrics, and crime incidence.
  • Spatial relationships: accessibility, travel time, trade routes, and movement corridors.
A map is uniquely powerful because it binds where (spatial position) directly with what (substantive attribute data).
Source concept: Torguson, Cartography: Thematic Map Design, cited in Lecture 5.
Analytical Mindset

04 — Map Design Changes What the Reader Notices

A map can use completely accurate, verified data and still lead readers to vastly different analytical conclusions based purely on design choices.

The cartographer or analyst controls critical design levers:

  • Geographic extent & bounding box: What is framed versus what is excluded.
  • Level of aggregation: State, county, tract, or block-group resolution.
  • Classification methodology: Natural breaks, quantiles, equal intervals, or standard deviations.
  • Symbology & visual variables: Color hue, lightness ramp, symbol area, and layer ordering.
Key Principle: Two maps can use the exact same underlying numbers and direct attention to opposing patterns. As an intelligence analyst, you must evaluate both the data and the design choices.
Geodesy & Coordinates

05 — The Earth, the Ellipsoid, and the Geoid

NASA Blue Marble: Composite of physical Earth observations.
The Geoid: Exaggerated gravitational equipotential surface.

Coordinates require reference surfaces: the physical Earth (irregular), the reference ellipsoid (smooth mathematical model), and the geoid (gravitational equipotential sea-level model). Sources: NASA Blue Marble & NASA SVS.

Coordinate Foundations

06 — Latitude and Longitude

Latitude measures north-south angular distance from the Equator (parallels). Longitude measures east-west angular distance from the Prime Meridian (meridians). A coordinate establishes location, not intent or causality.

Coordinate Formats

07 — Degrees, Minutes, Seconds, and Decimal Degrees

Traditional notation uses degrees, minutes, and seconds (DMS). Modern GIS and data platforms rely on numeric decimal degrees (DD). In the Western hemisphere, longitudes are expressed as negative values (e.g., -78.8698°).

Spatial Data Engineering

08 — Geocoding Connects an Address to a Coordinate

Geocoders match text descriptions against reference locator databases. Always perform visual QA/QC: verify whether candidate coordinates land in logical, expected locations before conducting analysis.
Spatial Integrity

09 — Geographic and Projected Coordinate Systems

GCS uses 3D angles (degrees) on an ellipsoid. PCS projects coordinates onto a 2D plane (meters or feet). Use GCS for storage/data exchange; use an appropriate PCS when measuring distance, area, or spatial proximity.

Map Projections

10 — From a Curved Earth to a Flat Map

Projection Tradeoffs: Flattening a curved sphere onto a 2D plane mathematically requires compromise. Every projection distorts shape, area, distance, or direction. (Open animation on YouTube)
Map Scale

11 — Zoom Changes Map Scale

Small Scale = Large area, generalized features, low detail. Large Scale = Small area, building/cadastral features, high detail. Remember: scale fraction 1/1,000,000 is small; 1/5,000 is large.

Cartographic Elements

12 — Three Ways to Express Scale

A graphic scale bar remains accurate when maps are resized, zoomed, or projected on screens. Verbal statements and fixed fractions are only valid at their original printed dimension.
Color & Semantics

13 — Correct Data Can Still Use a Poor Color Encoding

Question: Does red, purple, or black represent the higher unemployment rate? Using arbitrary categorical hues to represent an ordered, continuous variable forces readers to decode an unnatural visual riddle. Reference: BLS Dec 2008 / Jeffrey A. Shaffer critique.

Visual Encodings

14 — Match the Color Scheme to the Data and Task

Match the visual role of color to the mathematical structure of the data: Sequential for ordered quantities; Diverging for deviations from a baseline; Categorical for distinct qualitative groups.

Design Discipline

15 — A Sequential Ramp Makes Order Visible

Using a single perceptual hue with an ordered lightness ramp establishes an immediate cognitive rule: lighter = lower rate; darker = higher rate. The spatial pattern of the 2008 recession becomes instantly apparent. Source: BLS / Shaffer redesign.

Accessible Palette

16 — Grayscale Can Also Show Order

Color hue is not mandatory for quantitative mapping. Lightness alone communicates order clearly, prints reliably in black and white, and prevents color-vision deficiency barriers. Source: BLS / Shaffer redesign.

Comparative Analysis

17 — Same Data, Three Color Choices

The data rows and geographical boundaries are 100% identical. The difference in interpretation speed and perceptual accuracy is determined solely by cartographic color discipline.

Cognitive Traps

18 — Different Class Breaks Can Create a False Comparison

Both maps look similar at a glance, but their underlying break values differ substantially. Matching colors do not signify matching values. Analyst habit: Always inspect the legend before comparing maps.

Legend Integrity

19 — Read the Legends Before You Compare the Maps

To make a legitimate temporal or cross-regional comparison, hold class breaks, classification rules, color ramps, and geographic units strictly constant across all views.
Data Classification

20 — Why Classification Matters

A choropleth or color-coded thematic map groups continuous numeric observations into a manageable number of discrete visual bins (typically 4 to 6 classes).

Classification is the mathematical rule that sets those class boundaries.

The raw data remains unchanged. The classification method determines which areas are visually grouped together and which regional disparities are highlighted.

Sample series: 103, 109, 109, 115, 121, 123, 134, 171, 175, 178, 221, 246, 409, 450

Source dataset: Instructor Lecture 5 / Dr. Zachary Jared Bortolot.

Classification Methods

21 — Equal Interval

Divides the range (max - min) into classes of equal numeric width. Ideal for continuous, evenly distributed phenomena (e.g., temperature). Skewed data produces crowded lower bins and empty high bins.

Classification Methods

22 — Quantile

Distributes an equal number of geographic features into each class bin. Excellent for ranking (e.g., top 20% vs. bottom 20%). Warning: can group vastly different values together or split nearly identical values across bins.

Classification Methods

23 — Natural Breaks (Jenks)

Identifies natural cluster groupings and places class breaks where there are large gaps in the data array. Minimizes within-class variance while maximizing between-class differences. Breaks are data-specific.

Classification Methods

24 — Standard Deviation

Calculates class breaks using distance from the statistical mean (±1σ, ±2σ). Highlights geographic outliers and anomalies above or below a national/regional benchmark.

Analyst Checklist

27 — Classification Decision Checklist

Before accepting default software classifications in ArcGIS or Tableau, systematically verify:

  1. Data distribution shape: Normal bell curve, highly skewed, bimodal, or uniform?
  2. Outliers: Are there extreme values that distort equal interval widths?
  3. Feature distribution: Are classes populated with balanced counts or empty bins?
  4. Comparative requirement: Are you comparing maps across time periods or study regions?
  5. Analytical intent: Are you answering a ranking question, identifying clusters, or evaluating deviations from a norm?
The software calculates the mathematics; the analyst bears ethical and professional responsibility for the interpretation.
Map Composition

28 — Map Elements Have Specific Jobs

A professional analytical map is an integrated communication artifact composed of purposeful elements:

Title: Defines topic, geography, and timeframe.

Map Body: Primary analytical visual payload.

Legend: Decodes symbol classes and measurement units.

Scale: Communicates spatial dimension and distance.

Orientation: North arrow or coordinate graticule.

Metadata/Source: Provider, publication date, and boundaries.

Marginalia Precision

30 — Title, Legend, Insets, and Source Notes

  • Title: Unambiguously states What, Where, and When (e.g., "Median Household Income by Census Block Group — Rockingham County, VA (2026)").
  • Legend: Contains clear numeric breaks without overlapping ranges. Always state units ($USD, %, persons per sq mile).
  • Detail Inset: Magnifies dense urban nodes or complex intersections.
  • Location Inset: Situates the local study area within a statewide or national framework.
  • Source Note: Citations that allow the reader to independently verify the underlying data.
Symbology Standards

31 — Symbols Need a Consistent Visual Language

Standardized cartographic symbol palettes for transportation, hydrology, administrative boundaries, and terrain features. Symbols must remain legible at the map's target presentation scale. Source: USGS public domain.

Map Composition

32 — A Clean Map Shows Where Each Element Belongs

Balanced visual composition: avoid cluttering map margins. Place secondary metadata quietly while reserving primary visual real estate for the analytical map body.

Perceptual Hierarchy

33 — Visual Hierarchy: What Should the Reader Notice First?

Visual Hierarchy Rule: 1) Thematic analytical findings → 2) Study area boundary & key labels → 3) Background basemap context. Basemaps should support the story, never compete with it.

Thematic Typology

34 — Six Thematic Map Types

Choropleth, Dot Density, Proportional Symbol, Isarithmic, Cartogram, and Flow Map. Each type addresses a distinct analytical question.

Thematic Map Types

35 — Choropleth Map: Use a Relative Value by Area

Choropleth Golden Rule: Always map a normalized rate, ratio, percentage, index, or density. Never shade polygons by raw counts or population totals—larger geographic areas will falsely dominate visual attention simply due to land area.
Thematic Map Types

36 — Dot-Density / Dot-Distribution Map

Each white dot represents a discrete quantity (e.g., 1 dot = 7,500 people). Illustrates population clustering and sparse zones without being constrained by artificial administrative boundary polygons. Source: U.S. Census Bureau.

Thematic Map Types

37 — Proportional Symbol Map: Use Size for Totals

Proportional symbol maps scale marker area directly with absolute totals (e.g., total residents). This prevents land-area bias when mapping raw counts. Source: U.S. Census Bureau.

Thematic Map Types

38 — Isarithmic Map: Show a Continuous Field

Isolines connect points of equal value across continuous physical or statistical fields (e.g., elevation contours, precipitation, atmospheric pressure, seismic shaking intensity). Source: USGS public domain.

Thematic Map Types

39 — Cartogram: Resize Geography by a Value

Distorts geographic geometry so that polygon area is proportional to a chosen attribute (e.g., 1 square = 50,000 residents) rather than square miles of physical land. Source: U.S. Census Bureau.

Thematic Map Types

40 — Flow Map: Show Movement and Direction

Visualizes linear movement between origin and destination nodes. Vector line width typically denotes volume, weight, or capacity, while arrows indicate directionality. Source: NOAA / AOML.

Decision Matrix

41 — Match the Map Type to the Question

Analytical Question Recommended Map Type Primary Visual Variable
Where are rates, percentages, or densities high vs low? Choropleth Polygon color lightness
Where are individual events or people clustered? Dot Density Dot frequency / spatial concentration
Where are the largest raw totals or counts located? Proportional Symbol Marker area / diameter
How does a continuous surface vary across space? Isarithmic Isoline contours and filled bands
What does geography look like when sized by data? Cartogram Rescaled polygon area
How do commodities, people, or traffic move? Flow Map Line vector width & direction

Lecture Summary

44 — Main Takeaways

  1. Maps connect evidence through spatial relationships: Location binds disparate data streams.
  2. Design drives perception: Accurate data can still produce misleading impressions if classification or color are misused.
  3. Projections and scales matter: Small scale maps generalize; large scale maps reveal local detail. Every projection distorts some metric.
  4. Match encodings to metrics: Use choropleths for normalized rates/densities; use proportional symbols for raw counts.
  5. StoryMaps structure intelligence: Connect questions, spatial evidence, and narrative text in a clean, reproducible sequence.
