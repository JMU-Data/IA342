### Historical legibility tables

**Deborah T. Sharpe (1974). _The Psychology of Color and Design_. Nelson-Hall, p. 107.** [Book bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Psychology_of_Color_and_Design.html?id=LNN-AAAAMAAJ). The left table carries the printed attribution “Sharpe 1974, 107.” Book metadata is verified; the original p. 107 scan and study conditions have not been independently inspected.

**Hackl (1981), pp. 41–44.** This is the attribution printed beneath the right table. The full author name, publication title and publisher remain unverified. Do not manufacture a book title or replace this attribution with the instructor’s name. Treat this citation as incomplete until the original bibliography is available.

The two tables are preserved as **historical legibility rankings**. Their ordering differs, and neither table establishes a universal ranking of which color pairs people like. The activity asks about liking and readability separately, and calculates contemporary text contrast as a separate comparison. ## Scientific and accessibility references

**NASA Science. “Visible Light.”** [Original educational resource](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/). Basis for the approximate visible-spectrum range and the light/surface/perception demonstration. Local spectrum colors are display approximations, not calibrated wavelength measurements.

**National Eye Institute. “Color Blindness.”** [Original educational resource](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/color-blindness). Background on color-vision differences. The unidentified apple-photo comparison is no longer used as the lecture’s scientific simulation figure; the named-model comparison is used instead.

**National Eye Institute. “Color Blindness” and “Types of Color Vision Deficiency.”** [Overview](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/color-blindness) · [Types](https://www.nei.nih.gov/eye-health-information/eye-conditions-and-diseases/color-blindness/types-color-vision-deficiency). These pages support the red–green / blue–yellow distinction and the names protanopia, deuteranopia, protanomaly, deuteranomaly, tritanopia, and tritanomaly. The lecture’s `cvd-cone-model.svg` is an original teaching schematic: it illustrates overlapping S/M/L responses and the conceptual difference between dichromacy and anomalous trichromacy; it is not a measured spectral-sensitivity plot.

**Gustavo M. Machado, Manuel M. Oliveira and Leandro A. F. Fernandes (2009). “A Physiologically-based Model for Simulation of Color Vision Deficiency.” _IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics_ 15(6): 1291–1298.** [DOI: 10.1109/TVCG.2009.113](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2009.113). [Official Colorspacious implementation tutorial](https://colorspacious.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorial.html#simulating-colorblindness). The local comparison is an approximate, full-severity matrix simulation using linear RGB followed by sRGB encoding. It is not a medical test or an exact representation of every viewer’s experience. This CVD paper is **not** cited as the source of the HSV lesson.

**W3C. WCAG 2.2, Understanding SC 1.4.1: Use of Color; SC 1.4.3: Contrast (Minimum).** [Use of Color](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html) · [Contrast (Minimum)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html). Text-contrast checks are one criterion, not a whole-page accessibility certification or a requirement that all choropleth classes differ by the ordinary-text contrast ratio.

**Cynthia A. Brewer, Mark Harrower and The Pennsylvania State University. ColorBrewer 2.0.** [Original palette tool](https://colorbrewer2.org/). Sequential, diverging and qualitative palette exploration. “About five or fewer key categories” remains a practical classroom guideline, not a fixed biological limit.
