# IA342 Mini Project

**Exploring Your Assigned State with ArcGIS**

Individual project | ArcGIS Business Analyst and ArcGIS Dashboards

Due: Tuesday, Oct 6, 2026, at the time specified in Canvas.

Submission: One public dashboard viewing URL in Canvas.

**LATE SUBMISSIONS AND LATE RESUBMISSIONS ARE NOT ACCEPTED.**

## 1. Project Goal

Create a public, interactive dashboard that explores a clear topic in your randomly assigned U.S. state. This project combines the thematic mapping skills from Lab 3 with one analysis method and the dashboard skills from Lab 4; you do not need to repeat every task from those labs.

Choose a topic that interests you and can be explored using the data available through your course access to ArcGIS Business Analyst. Your title and introduction should identify the topic and explain what you want the viewer to understand.

For example, a topic could be “Crime and Income in [Assigned State]” or “Population and Housing Patterns in [Assigned State].” You do not need to phrase your topic as a formal research question.

Your dashboard must contain two maps, one data widget, and a brief write-up. These elements must work together to communicate your topic, rather than present unrelated information.

## 2. Assigned State and Data

Use your randomly assigned state as the study area and compare its counties or county-equivalent areas. Use a consistent geographic level across the project.

Choose two relevant variables from the data available through your course access to Business Analyst. You may use additional variables, but they are not required. Both selected variables must contribute meaningfully to the project; they do not both have to appear in every map or widget.

Choose variables because they help explore your topic—not simply because they appeared in the instructor’s example. Identify their names, sources, years, and units in your write-up or source notes.

You are not required to download external data, convert CSV files, geocode addresses, or create a customized report. A separate StoryMap or infographic is also not required. Saving and exporting your completed map and analysis layers for use in the dashboard is still necessary.

## 3. Required Dashboard Components

### Map 1 — Thematic Map

Create one thematic map showing a variable relevant to your topic. Choose either a choropleth map or a proportional symbol map, depending on the data.

Use choropleth mapping for appropriate area-level measures, such as rates, percentages, densities, or median household income. Use proportional symbols for counts or totals, such as total population. Do not use raw counts in a choropleth map simply because that is the default style.

The map must have a clear purpose. A reader should understand what is mapped, what the symbols or colors mean, and how the map contributes to your topic.

### Map 2 — Analysis-Result Map

Complete ONE analysis and display its result as your second map. You may use **Benchmark comparisons, Suitability analysis, or Nearby analysis**. You may also use another ArcGIS Business Analyst analysis workflow if it better fits your topic and you can clearly explain its purpose, configuration, and result. Any appropriate analysis option is eligible for full credit.

**Benchmark comparisons:** Identify the variable or variables being compared, the benchmark, and what above/below the benchmark means. State the reference precisely—for example, the median of county-level values, rather than calling it the statewide value.

**Suitability analysis:** Use your selected variables as criteria. Explain the influence and weight of each criterion, why those choices fit your topic, and what a higher score means. Use percentage weights totaling 100%. Two criteria are sufficient.

**Nearby analysis:** Use this option when proximity to points of interest is relevant to your topic. Explain the sites you analyzed, the point list or POI category you selected, the distance or area settings, and what the resulting count, distance, or density measures mean.

Choose the analysis method that best supports your topic. Benchmark comparisons evaluates areas against a reference, suitability analysis combines selected criteria into a score or ranking, and Nearby analysis examines proximity between sites and points of interest.

Do not treat an analysis result as proof of a relationship between variables unless the method actually supports that conclusion. For example, combining crime and income into a suitability score describes areas under your chosen criteria; the combined score alone does not establish that crime and income are correlated.

Your two maps must serve different purposes. Displaying the same map twice does not satisfy the requirement.

### Widget — Additional Visualization and Interaction

Add one data-driven widget, such as a serial/bar chart, indicator, or gauge. Select a variable or analysis result that contributes to your topic.

Give the widget a clear title and appropriate labels or units. Make it clear what is being displayed—for example, a selected county’s value, a total, or an average across the displayed counties.

The widget must respond to a meaningful dashboard interaction. For example, selecting counties on a map or changing the map extent can filter the widget to show the corresponding data. Configure the relevant layer-selection or map-extent action and its target.

Include one short instruction explaining how to use the interaction. Test that the widget changes correctly and that the viewer can clear the selection or return to the initial view.

A map that only pans, zooms, or opens pop-ups, without updating the widget, does not satisfy the interaction requirement.

This widget also satisfies the additional visualization requirement. You do not need another chart or infographic. A legend, filter control, or static screenshot alone does not count as the required data widget.

### Write-Up — Brief, Specific, and Integrated

Place your explanations directly in the dashboard using Rich text, short captions, and source notes. Do not submit a separate essay.

Aim for approximately 150–250 words total. This is a length guideline, not a reason to omit required information. Short captions and source notes count toward the write-up.

Your writing must answer the following:

* Topic and purpose: What is your topic? Why did you choose it, and what do you want the viewer to explore or understand?
* Data: Which variables did you use? Identify their sources, years, geographic level, and units or metric definitions.
* Maps and analysis: What does each map show? How does each contribute to the topic? Explain the key settings for your chosen analysis—for example, your benchmark; your suitability criteria, influences, and weights; your Nearby analysis sites, POI category, and distance/area settings; or the equivalent configuration for another analysis workflow.
* Widget and interaction: What does the widget display? What should the viewer do, and how does the widget respond?
* Design choices: Briefly justify your map type, color scheme, and classification method or symbol sizing.
* Findings and limitation: What did you learn? Give at least two specific observations supported by place names and values from your results, and identify one relevant limitation.

You may distribute this information beside the relevant elements rather than place everything in one paragraph. Do not merely write “This is my choropleth map” or “I used suitability analysis.” Explain what the visual actually communicates.

Your name, professional contact information, LinkedIn profile, and portfolio link are optional and are not graded.

## 4. Design Expectations

Apply the map-design, color-theory, and visualization principles discussed in class to both maps and the widget.

Color: Use sequential colors for ordered low-to-high values. Use a diverging scheme when emphasizing values on opposite sides of a meaningful reference, and make that reference clear.

Classification and symbols: For classified maps, choose a reasonable classification method and number of classes. For an unclassed proportional-symbol map, explain the size encoding instead of adding unnecessary classes.

Map elements and layout: Both maps should have clear titles, accessible legends, readable labels or pop-ups, and scale bars enabled where supported. Keep the maps large enough to interpret. Use readable text, appropriate contrast, consistent formatting, and a layout that makes the relationship among the maps, widget, and explanations easy to follow.

Design is evaluated within each component. Attractive colors cannot compensate for an inappropriate map type or an incorrectly calculated value.

## 5. Public Access — Required for Credit

Your submitted dashboard must work for a viewer who is not signed in to ArcGIS. Share the dashboard and all required web maps and layers with Everyone (public). Sharing the dashboard alone does not make its supporting maps and layers public.

**If the submitted dashboard is not publicly accessible, the entire project receives 0/100.**

**If the dashboard opens but a required map, widget, or other component is not publicly accessible, that component receives 0 points. The remaining accessible components are graded normally.**

Being visible to you—or to the instructor while signed in—does not satisfy the public-access requirement. Screenshots do not replace an inaccessible component.

Before submitting, open the viewing URL in a private/incognito window. Confirm that both maps, the widget, the write-up, and the interaction work without a sign-in prompt. When a map or widget is blocked, check the sharing settings of its supporting web map and layers, then test again.

## 6. Submission and Deadline

Submit one public dashboard viewing URL in Canvas. Do not submit a builder/edit URL, a Business Analyst project URL, screenshots, or a PDF instead.

Complete your content, interaction, and public-sharing settings before the deadline. Leave the dashboard and supporting items available for grading.

**LATE SUBMISSIONS AND LATE RESUBMISSIONS ARE NOT ACCEPTED.**

## 7. Grading Rubric — 100 Points

Each required item is worth 25 points. Within each item, grading considers accuracy, contribution to the topic, and effective design—not simply whether the item is present.

| Item | What is evaluated | Points |
| :---- | :---- | :---: |
| Map 1: Thematic map | Correct assigned state and geographic level; appropriate variable and map type; effective contribution to the topic; suitable colors, classification or symbol sizing, legend, and readable presentation. | 25 |
| Map 2: Analysis-result map | Appropriate and correctly configured analysis; meaningful use of the selected data, sites, or criteria; results that support the topic; accurate interpretation of the analysis output (such as benchmark values, suitability scores, or nearby count/distance/density measures); effective map design. | 25 |
| Widget and interaction | 15 points: relevant and correctly calculated information, clear labels and units, and effective visual design. 10 points: a working interaction that meaningfully updates the widget. | 25 |
| Write-up | Clear topic and purpose; identifiable data; explanations of both maps and the widget; analysis settings and design rationale; specific, supported findings and a limitation. Concise and well organized. | 25 |
| **Total** | **Four required items, each worth 25 points.** | **100** |

Public-access rules apply before these items are graded. An inaccessible dashboard receives 0/100; an inaccessible component receives 0 for that item.

A publicly accessible widget that displays correctly but does not respond to the required interaction earns no interaction points (0/10). An inaccessible widget receives 0/25 for the entire widget item.
